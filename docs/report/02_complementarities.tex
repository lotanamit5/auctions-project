\section{Complementarities between the items}
\label{sec:eps}

\subsection{Motivation}

The paper assumes additive valuations and names ``complementary/substitutable preferences over
the items'' as a direction for future work (its Section~7). The simplest departure is a single
complementarity term: agent $i$ with type $(v,1-v)$ values the bundle $(z_1,z_2)$ at $u_v(z_1,z_2)=v\,z_1+(1-v)\,z_2+\varepsilon\,z_1z_2$ where $\varepsilon\ge0\ \text{is common knowledge, and }\ v\ \text{private}.$
% \[
% u_v(z_1,z_2)=v\,z_1+(1-v)\,z_2+\varepsilon\,z_1z_2,\qquad \varepsilon\ge0\ \text{common knowledge},\ v\ \text{private}.
% \]

For example, suppose two firms divide one unit each of production and distribution
capacity. A firm with $v=0.9$ values production much more than distribution, while a
firm with $v=0.1$ has the opposite priority. When $\varepsilon=0.5$, assigning
production to the first firm and distribution to the second creates welfare
$0.9+0.9=1.8$, whereas giving both capacities to one firm creates $1.5$.
By contrast, if both firms have $v=0.9$, the same split creates welfare $1$,
whereas pooling both capacities at either firm creates $1.5$.
Thus the welfare-maximizing rule must respond to whether preferences agree or
conflict.

\subsection{Results: the first best and LIP under complementarities}

\begin{proposition}[first best welfare]\label{prop:fb} For types $x, y \in [0, 1],$ the maximum feasible social welfare is
$\mathrm{OPT}(x,y)=1+\max\{\varepsilon,\,|x-y|\}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $(z_1,z_2)$ be agent 1's allocation; agent 2 receives
$(1-z_1,1-z_2)$. Total welfare is
\[
{W(z_1,z_2)=1+(x-y)(z_1-z_2)
 +\varepsilon\big[z_1z_2+(1-z_1)(1-z_2)\big].
}\]
{This function is affine in either coordinate with the other fixed, hence a
maximum on $[0,1]^2$ occurs at a corner. Allocating both goods to one agent
produces $1+\varepsilon$; splitting them according to their relative marginal
values produces $1+|x-y|$. A randomized allocation is a mixture of deterministic
allocations and cannot improve on their maximum. Thus
$\mathrm{OPT}(x,y)=1+\max\{\varepsilon,|x-y|\}$} .
%     Under the naive rule a dictator who
% reports $r$ receives the frontier point at spend $t_0(r)=\operatorname{clip}((r(a+2b)-b)/a,0,1)$;
% $r\mapsto t_0(r)$ is onto $[0,1]$, and her report is irrelevant in the other branch. Her dominant
% strategy is therefore any $r$ with $t_0(r)=t^\star(x)$, and the realized allocation is that of the
% $\varepsilon$-aware mechanism. For the misreport bound, write $F^\varepsilon_x(t)=F^0_x(t)+\varepsilon P(t)$;
% $t_0(x)$ maximizes $F^0_x$, so for any $s$, $F^\varepsilon_x(s)-F^\varepsilon_x(t_0(x))\le\varepsilon[P(s)-P(t_0(x))]\le\varepsilon\max P=\varepsilon Q(\tfrac12)^2$,
% and the coin halves it.
\end{proof}

% Welfare is bilinear in agent~1's bundle, so a corner of $[0,1]^2$ is optimal: either the additive
% split ($1+|x-y|$) or \emph{pooling} - both items to one agent ($1+\varepsilon$). Two consequences
% frame everything that follows.
Notice that for $\varepsilon\ge1$ the report-free mechanism ``fair coin, the
winner takes both items'' (\emph{take-all}) is first best at every profile, so the interesting
regime is $0<\varepsilon<1$. And the efficient rule now depends on \emph{agreement}: it pools when
the agents want the same item and splits when they do not.

\begin{proposition}[fixed menus stay strategy-proof]\label{prop:menu}
Any mechanism in which a fair coin picks a dictator who receives her favourite element of a
fixed menu of (possibly random) bundles, the other agent receiving the remainder, is
strategy-proof for every utility function, in particular for every $\varepsilon$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $M$ be the dictator's report-independent menu of lotteries, and let the
mechanism choose an expected-utility maximizer in $M$ for each reported type.
If the agent is dictator, truthful reporting obtains her preferred lottery in
$M$, whereas a misreport can only select another element of $M$. If the other
agent is dictator, the first agent's report has no effect. Since the lottery
that picks the dictator is independent of both reports, truthful reporting is
a dominant strategy. The argument does not require additive valuations and
therefore continues to hold in the presence of complementarities.
\end{proof}

This gives the \emph{$\varepsilon$-aware LIP$(a)$}: the menu is the paper's LIP frontier
$\{(Q(t),Q(1-t)):t\in[0,1]\}$, fixed by the price schedule and not by the reports, and the
dictator chooses a bundle maximizing her utility for the reported type and the public
parameter $\varepsilon$. The three results below separate its optimization properties,
the incentives under the original additive rule, and the role of lottery correlation.

\begin{proposition}[$\varepsilon$-aware LIP: optimization and incentives]
\label{prop:eps_lip_properties}
Suppose the LIP frontier $Q:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ is increasing and strictly concave,
with $Q(t)>0$ for interior $t$. For every $\varepsilon\ge0$, the dictator's objective
\[
F_v(t)=vQ(t)+(1-v)Q(1-t)+\varepsilon Q(t)Q(1-t)
\]
is strictly concave in $t$. Its maximizer $t^\star(v)$ is unique and
nondecreasing in $v$. Choosing this maximizer from the fixed LIP lottery menu
is strategy-proof.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Put $P(t)=Q(t)Q(1-t)$. On the interior of the frontier,
\[
P''(t)=Q''(t)Q(1-t)-2Q'(t)Q'(1-t)+Q(t)Q''(1-t)\le0.
\]
In fact the strict concavity of the positive-weight additive terms (and of $P$
on the interior) makes $F_v$ strictly concave; continuity gives a unique
maximizer on the compact interval. Furthermore,
\[
\frac{\partial^2 F_v}{\partial t\,\partial v}
=Q'(t)+Q'(1-t)>0.
\]
Thus $F_v$ has increasing differences in $(t,v)$, so its unique maximizer is
nondecreasing in the type $v$. The menu of allocation lotteries is fixed
independently of either report. Truthful reporting selects the dictator's
most-preferred member of this menu; a misreport can only select another
menu member. A non-dictator's report has no effect, and dictator selection
is report-independent. Hence truthful reporting is weakly dominant.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Manipulability of the additive LIP rule]
\label{prop:additive-lip}

Consider applying the original additive LIP report-to-allocation
rule of Guo and Conitzer to agents whose true utilities exhibit
complementarities. In particular, suppose the dictator reports
$r\in[0,1]$, and the mechanism selects $t_0(r)\in\arg\max_{t\in[0,1]}
\left\{rQ(t)+(1-r)Q(1-t)\right\},$
% \[
% t_0(r)\in\arg\max_{t\in[0,1]}
% \left\{rQ(t)+(1-r)Q(1-t)\right\},
% \]
while the dictator's true utility from allocation $t$ is $F_v^\varepsilon(t)=vQ(t)+(1-v)Q(1-t)
+\varepsilon Q(t)Q(1-t).$
% \[
% F_v^\varepsilon(t)=vQ(t)+(1-v)Q(1-t)
% +\varepsilon Q(t)Q(1-t).
% \]

For $\varepsilon>0$, this report-to-allocation rule is not
strategy-proof in general. Moreover, since the mapping
$r\mapsto t_0(r)$ covers the entire LIP frontier, the dictator
can induce their preferred allocation $t^\star(v)\in\arg\max_{t\in[0,1]}F_v^\varepsilon(t)$
% \[
% t^\star(v)\in\arg\max_{t\in[0,1]}F_v^\varepsilon(t)
% \]
through an appropriate report. Under uniform random dictatorship, the ex-ante gain from
an optimal misreport is bounded above by $\Delta U\leq \varepsilon Q(\tfrac12)^2/2$ ($\approx0.26\,\varepsilon$ for $a=2$).
% \[
% \Delta U\leq \frac{\varepsilon Q(1/2)^2}{2}.
% \]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The additive allocation rule maps a report $r\in[0,1]$ to
\[
t_0(r)=\operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(\frac{r(a+2b)-b}{a}\right),
\]
where $a,b$ are the LIP frontier parameters. As $r$ varies from $0$ to
$1$, this continuous clipped affine map reaches every $t\in[0,1]$.
Consequently, a dictator can induce any frontier point, including the
maximizer $t^\star(v)$ of her true utility $F_v$.
The original rule generally chooses $t_0(v)$ rather than $t^\star(v)$;
when these differ, misreporting to induce $t^\star(v)$ strictly improves
utility. For example, for an interior type $v$ whose additive optimizer
$t_0(v)$ is neither $1/2$ nor a boundary point, the derivative of the
complementarity contribution is $\varepsilon P'(t_0(v))$, which is
nonzero because $P$ is symmetric and strictly concave, so $P'(t)=0$ only at $t=1/2$.

Let $F_v^0$ denote the additive objective. Since $t_0(v)$ maximizes $F_v^0$,
for every available $t$,
\[
F_v^\varepsilon(t)-F_v^\varepsilon(t_0(v))
\le\varepsilon\bigl[P(t)-P(t_0(v))\bigr]
\le\varepsilon Q(\tfrac12)^2.
\]
The last inequality uses $P(t)\le P(1/2)=Q(1/2)^2$ for the symmetric
concave frontier. The agent is dictator with probability $1/2$ and has
no influence when she is not dictator, giving the stated ex-ante bound.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Nested LIP lotteries]\label{prop:nested_lip}
For any fixed marginal allocation probabilities $m_1,m_2$, coupling the
two item allocations through a common uniform random variable weakly
increases the expected complementarity term for \emph{both} agents relative
to independent item lotteries, without changing their additive expected
utilities. If the resulting nested lottery menu is fixed, choosing one's
preferred lottery from it remains strategy-proof. Under the symmetric
LIP frontier, nested LIP attains first-best welfare $1+\varepsilon$ for
$\varepsilon\ge1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $U\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]$, and give item $j$ to the dictator
if $U<m_j$. The dictator receives both items with probability
$\min(m_1,m_2)$, while the non-dictator receives both with probability
$1-\max(m_1,m_2)$. Independent item lotteries instead give the respective
probabilities $m_1m_2$ and $(1-m_1)(1-m_2)$. The inequalities
\[
\min(m_1,m_2)\ge m_1m_2,\qquad
1-\max(m_1,m_2)\ge(1-m_1)(1-m_2)
\]
prove the welfare comparison for $\varepsilon\ge0$. Since the new menu
is report-independent, the fixed-menu strategy-proofness argument applies.

For nested LIP, write $m_1=Q(t)$ and $m_2=Q(1-t)$. The dictator maximizes
\[
G_v(t)=vQ(t)+(1-v)Q(1-t)
       +\varepsilon\min\{Q(t),Q(1-t)\}.
\]
The symmetric concave frontier has $Q'\ge0$ decreasing. For $t<1/2$,
\[
G_v'(t)=(v+\varepsilon)Q'(t)-(1-v)Q'(1-t)
\ge Q'(t)-Q'(1-t)\ge0
\]
when $\varepsilon\ge1$. For $t>1/2$,
\[
G_v'(t)=vQ'(t)-(1-v+\varepsilon)Q'(1-t)
\le Q'(t)-Q'(1-t)\le0.
\]
Hence a dictator chooses $t=1/2$ (possibly among tied choices), at
which $m_1=m_2$. The nested lottery allocates both items to one agent
or both to the other with probability one. Every realization therefore
has welfare $1+\varepsilon$, which equals the first-best value when
$\varepsilon\ge1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:fb}).
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Numerical competitive-ratio certificates (separate from the propositions).}
The propositions above are analytic; the reported competitive ratios
require additional numerical bounds. On a type cell
$[x_0,x_0+h]\times[y_0,y_0+h]$, truthful optimization supplies
$u_x(q(x))\ge u_x(q(x_0))$, whose right side is affine in $x$ and
can be bounded using the endpoints. Monotonicity of the frontier choices
gives $q_1(x)\le q_1(x_0+h)$ and $q_2(x)\le q_2(x_0)$.
One resulting lower bound on the non-dictator's utility is
\[
y_0[1-q_1(x_0+h)]+(1-y_0-h)[1-q_2(x_0)]
+\varepsilon[1-q_1(x_0+h)][1-q_2(x_0)].
\]
A symmetric bound applies when the other agent is dictator. On cells
with $x\ge y$, divide the total welfare lower bound by the upper bound
$1+\max\{\varepsilon,x_0+h-y_0\}$ on first-best welfare.
For nested lotteries the last product is replaced by
$1-\max\{q_1(x_0+h),q_2(x_0)\}$. Refining the grid gives lower
bounds, and evaluated profiles provide upper bounds. For $\varepsilon$-aware LIP(2) the certified ratios (to $10^{-4}$) at
$\varepsilon=0.01,0.1,0.5,1,2$ are $0.8308,\ 0.8403,\ 0.8100,\ 0.7652,\ 0.7153$.

\subsection{Extending the bounds}

\paragraph{Upper bound on every strategy-proof mechanism.} With lotteries, utilities are no
longer linear in the expected bundle, so the LP of the paper's Theorem~1 does not apply verbatim. But because
$u_v$ is bilinear, each agent's utility from a lottery depends only on the moments
$(m_1,m_2,m_{12})=(\mathbb E z_1,\mathbb E z_2,\mathbb E z_1z_2)$, and the attainable triples are
exactly the McCormick envelope $\{m_{12}\ge0,\ m_{12}\ge m_1+m_2-1,\ m_{12}\le m_1,\ m_{12}\le m_2\}$
(Appendix~\ref{app:eps}, P7). The best strategy-proof mechanism on a type grid is therefore
again an LP, in the three moments per profile, and its value bounds every randomised
strategy-proof mechanism. The bound rises quickly with $\varepsilon$: $0.873$ ($\varepsilon=0.1$),
$0.951$ ($0.5$), $0.984$ ($0.8$), $1$ ($\varepsilon\ge1$) (grid $N=80$).

\paragraph{Upper bound on fixed-menu mechanisms.} The best fixed-menu mechanism on a grid is also
an LP (the menu items are the variables; incentive and welfare constraints are linear in them),
and the following theorem bounds it in closed form:
\begin{theorem}[fixed-menu bound]\label{thm:menu_bound}
Every fixed-menu mechanism is at most
$\min\{\rho_A(\varepsilon),\rho_B(\varepsilon)\}$-competitive, where
\[
\rho_A(\varepsilon)=\frac{5+2\varepsilon}{6}\ (\varepsilon\le\tfrac12),\qquad
\rho_B(\varepsilon)=\frac{1+\varepsilon}{1+2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^2}.
\]
$\rho_B$ is attained for every $\varepsilon\ge\tfrac12$ by the two-item menu ``the dictator takes a
share $s^\star=(1-\varepsilon^2)/(1+2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^2)$ of her preferred item'' ($s^\star=3/7$
and $\rho_B=6/7$ at $\varepsilon=\tfrac12$).
\end{theorem}
The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:eps}) uses only three or four type profiles and no incentive
constraint. The menu LP, itself an upper bound, coincides with it at $\varepsilon=0$ and for
$\varepsilon\ge0.24$, and is lower by up to $0.018$ in between (at $\varepsilon\approx0.17$, where
$\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ cross), so there the theorem is not the binding bound. At $\varepsilon=0$ the bound is $5/6$; it holds for every mixture of two one-sided rules,
not only for menus, and an explicit menu attains it (Appendix~\ref{app:sd}). For additive
valuations, then, LIP($a^\star$) is within $0.004$ of the best dictatorial mechanism, which is in turn
within $0.003$ of the LP bound: restricting to dictatorial mechanisms costs almost nothing.
With complementarities two features are worth noting:
the optimal dictator is \emph{modest} - she leaves more than half of everything to the other
agent, because the other agent's complementarity gain is worth more than her own extra share -
and nested LIP with the best $a$ tracks $\rho_B$ almost exactly for $\varepsilon\ge\tfrac12$, so
within the menu class the paper's construction remains essentially optimal.

Figure~\ref{fig:eps_overview} summarises the section. Three things stand out. (i) The gap between
the dashed and the black curve - the \emph{price of dictatorship} - is about $0.1$ at
$\varepsilon=\tfrac12$ ($0.857$ vs $0.951$), far larger than in the additive model, where it is at most
$0.003$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:menu_bound} at $\varepsilon=0$). (ii) The worst case has moved: in the additive model identical types were the
easy case (ratio~1); with complementarities they become the hard one, since the first best pools
and a dictator splits. (iii) For $\varepsilon\ge1$ everything collapses to the trivial take-all. The
natural next question is whether a mechanism that reacts to agreement between the two reports
can close the gap; Section~\ref{sec:smd} gives one.
